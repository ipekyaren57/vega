The problem

On long missions to the Moon and Mars, astronauts carry much of the responsibility for spotting changes in their own health: sleep loss, bone loss, cardiovascular strain, behavioral health. They often do it while busy, tired, or minutes away from any reply from Earth. More dashboards and more alarms add stress to an already demanding job.

Our solution: one band, three ways to reach you

💪 Arm display

A curved screen on the forearm. A status strip on top shows the single most important message; a clickable body map and the 3 values that matter most right now.

〰 Haptic pulse

When hands and eyes are busy, e.g. during a spacewalk, a gentle pulse on the wrist comes before any sound or light: “take a look when you can”.

🎧 VEGA voice

"Vega, how is my heart?" Hands stay free. A short morning chat logs sleep and mood without a form.

🌍 Earth link

Telemetry reaches flight surgeons automatically, with realistic signal delay for the ISS, a Moon base and Mars transit.

How it works: an adaptive, low-stress interface

Nominal: minimal information, nothing competes for attention.
Attention: the view simplifies, VEGA explains why in a calm tone and suggests one action (“Ease your pace, you have time”).
Critical: one instruction, one focus (“Stop task, hold position”). Earth and the EVA partner are notified automatically.

Every change is announced, and the astronaut can always say “show all”, so control stays with the crew. Trends and predictions (“at this rate your sleep leaves the normal range in 2 days”) help catch problems early.

Design decisions

  • Inspired by adaptive cockpits: information is prioritized by context instead of shown all at once.
  • Fits everyone: a rigid curved display module plus a stretchable strap, sized from the smallest wrists (~14 cm) up to ~20 cm.
  • One device, focused: we deliberately dropped a helmet display and focused on a single band, so there is one place to look and nothing new inside the helmet.
  • Glove-friendly: large touch targets, with voice and haptic pulses as the main channels during EVA.
  • Distance from Earth: on Mars, a message can take up to ~20 minutes one way, so VEGA gives the first response on board and Earth follows.
  • Privacy: only the mood score is shared with Earth, never the astronaut's words; voice is never recorded.
  • Guidance, not just numbers: every indicator explains why it matters in space, how to measure it, when to tell Earth and what helps.

Built on NASA research

  • NASA Human Research Program, five hazards of human spaceflight: every body region in VEGA is tagged with the hazards that affect it.
  • NASA HRP, Risk of Spaceflight-Induced Bone Changes: 1–1.5 % bone density lost per month on 4–6-month missions, used for VEGA's bone estimate and exercise guidance.
  • NASA/NSBRI-funded astronaut sleep study (Barger et al., The Lancet Neurology, 2014): ISS crews averaged just over 6 h of sleep vs. 8.5 h scheduled, used for sleep thresholds and messages.
  • NASA HRP information on vision changes in long-duration astronauts, used for the eye region.
  • Future work: personal baselines from the NASA Open Science Data Repository.

What's next

Personal baselines learned per astronaut, a version of the band sized to fit over the spacesuit sleeve, and alerts shared with the EVA partner's band. The same calm-by-default principle could help people in other isolated, high-stress places: polar stations, submarines, remote clinics.
